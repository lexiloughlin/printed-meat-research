Technology

How 3D-printed meat works

Ground meat is easy to imitate. Whole cuts are hard because steak has structure: muscle fibers with fat running through them. Printing builds that structure layer by layer.

1
IngredientsPlant protein and fat, mycoprotein, or living animal cells
2
Printable "ink"Mixed into a paste (or bio-ink) that flows through a nozzle and then holds its shape
3
Digital modelSoftware maps where "muscle" and "fat" go to create marbling
4
PrintThe printer deposits both materials layer by layer
5
FinishPlant-based: set and package. Cultivated: incubate so cells grow into tissue
Plant-based printing

On sale today

Two pastes, one acting like muscle and one like fat, are layered together. Steakholder Foods calls this Fused Paste Layering; Revo Foods calls its version 3D Structuring and says the milder process helps preserve nutrients.

Cultivated bioprinting

Still in the lab

Living cow cells are printed and then grown. In 2021 Aleph Farms and the Technion made a ribeye prototype with real muscle and fat plus a vascular-like system to feed thicker tissue.

What the lab says: In a 2021 Foods study, xanthan gum made the best soy-based ink (guar gum wouldn't print at all). At 20% infill the "steak" cracked and dried out when fried; at 80–100% it stayed undercooked inside. 60% infill hit the sweet spot.

Market

How big is the market? It depends who you ask.

Paid market reports are often quoted as fact. These two disagree by about 61 times, even though one covers plant- and cell-based printed meat and the other only plant-based.

Published market-size estimates (US$ billions)

PublisherScopeBaseForecastCAGR
Exactitude Consultancy3D printed meat$178.64B (2023)$504.88B (2030)16%
Research Nester3D printing plant-based meat$2.92B (2024)$16.41B (2037)14.2%
Analyst take: Plant-based meat as a whole is under 1% of meat sales, purchases fell 10% in 2025, and only 11% of US households buy it. A $178B printed-meat market doesn't fit that picture, so that figure is likely measuring something much broader.

A 2025 study of 571 consumers in France, the Czech Republic and Ireland found people were less willing to try printed cultivated steak than other printed foods like pasta or sweets. Taste and price topped the list of what would change their minds.
Regulation

Texas banned it. Sort of.

Several states have acted on cultivated (cell-grown) meat. Texas SB 261 took effect September 1, 2025: a two-year ban on producing and selling it, with fines up to $25,000 per day.

Sale ban (3)

  • Texas · SB 261
  • Florida
  • Montana · HB 401

Labeling rules (3)

  • South Carolina · SB 103
  • South Dakota · HB 1022
  • Utah · HB 138

Study (1)

  • North Dakota · HB 1151
Court fight: Wildtype and UPSIDE Foods, with the Institute for Justice, sued Texas, arguing the ban is protectionism that violates the Commerce Clause. In January 2026 a federal judge let that claim proceed but denied a preliminary injunction, so the ban stays in effect for now.

Key nuance: these laws target cells, not printers. A plant-based printed steak is legal to sell in Texas.
